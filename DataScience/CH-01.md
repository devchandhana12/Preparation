## 1. Introduction

Descriptive Statistics is the branch of statistics used to summarize, organize, and understand data.

Before building an ML model, a Data Scientist should understand:

- **Central tendency:** Where is the data centered?
- **Dispersion:** How spread out are the values?
- **Distribution:** What is the shape of the data?
- **Position:** Where does an observation stand relative to others?
- **Relationships:** How do multiple variables behave together?

**Production principle:** Always define the metric and unit of analysis. Average watch time per video is not the same as average daily watch time per user.

---

## 2. Mean

The arithmetic mean is the average of all observations.

$$
\bar{x}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i}{n}
$$

Example:

Data: `10, 20, 30, 40, 50`

$$
\bar{x}=\frac{150}{5}=30
$$

**Advantages:**
- Easy to calculate.
- Uses every observation.
- Useful for aggregation and statistical analysis.

**Disadvantages:**
- Sensitive to outliers.
- Can misrepresent the typical observation in skewed data.

Example: Adding `150` to the dataset increases the mean to `50`, even though most observations are much smaller.

**Production note:** For distributed data, calculate the overall mean using global sums and counts. Do not simply average partition means when partition sizes differ.

---

## 3. Median

The median is the middle value after sorting observations.

For an odd number of observations, select the middle observation.

For an even number, average the two middle observations.

Example:

`10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 150`

$$
Median=\frac{30+40}{2}=35
$$

**Advantages:**
- Resistant to extreme values.
- Useful for skewed distributions.
- Often more representative of a typical salary, transaction, or watch-time value.

**Limitation:** It does not capture the total magnitude of observations or explain tail behavior.

---

## 4. Mode

Mode is the most frequently occurring value.

Example:

`10, 20, 20, 20, 30, 40`

Mode = `20`.

Types:

- **Unimodal:** One most frequent value.
- **Bimodal:** Two values share the highest frequency.
- **Multimodal:** Several values share the highest frequency.

Mode is especially useful for categorical data.

Example: If Mobile is the most frequently used device category, Mobile is the mode.

---

## 5. Variance

Variance measures the average squared deviation of observations from their mean.

### Population variance

$$
\sigma^2=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu)^2}{N}
$$

Example:

Data: `20, 30, 40`

Mean = 30.

| Value | Deviation | Squared deviation |
|---|---:|---:|
| 20 | -10 | 100 |
| 30 | 0 | 0 |
| 40 | +10 | 100 |

$$
Variance=\frac{100+0+100}{3}=66.67
$$

**Why square deviations?**

1. Prevent positive and negative deviations from cancelling.
2. Give more weight to large deviations.
3. Produce a useful mathematical measure of dispersion.

Variance uses squared measurement units.

---

## 6. Standard Deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance.

$$
\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}
$$

Example:

$$
\sigma=\sqrt{66.67}=8.16
$$

Unlike variance, standard deviation uses the original measurement unit.

### Interpretation

- **Low SD:** Observations are relatively close to the mean.
- **High SD:** Observations are more widely spread around the mean.

Two datasets can have identical means but very different standard deviations.

**Important:** Standard deviation is not exactly the average absolute distance from the mean.

---

## 7. Population vs Sample Variance

A population contains all observations relevant to the statistical question.

A sample is a subset used to estimate population properties.

### Population variance

$$
\sigma^2=\frac{\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}{N}
$$

### Sample variance

$$
s^2=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}
$$

For sampled observations `20, 30, 40`:

$$
s^2=\frac{200}{3-1}=100
$$

### Why n − 1?

Because the sample mean is calculated using the same observations, the deviations must sum to zero.

Only `n − 1` deviations can vary freely.

Dividing by `n − 1` corrects the downward bias in the usual population-variance estimate under standard random-sampling assumptions.

This correction is known as **Bessel's correction**.

---

## 8. Range

Range measures the difference between maximum and minimum observations.

$$
Range=Maximum-Minimum
$$

Example:

`10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 150`

$$
Range=150-10=140
$$

**Advantages:**
- Easy to calculate.
- Describes the full observed span.

**Disadvantages:**
- Extremely sensitive to outliers.
- Ignores the distribution of values between the extremes.

---

## 9. Quartiles

Quartiles divide ordered observations into four approximately equal parts.

- **Q1:** First quartile, approximately the 25th percentile.
- **Q2:** Second quartile, the median.
- **Q3:** Third quartile, approximately the 75th percentile.

Example:

`10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 150`

Using the median-of-halves method:

$$
Q1=\frac{20+30}{2}=25
$$

$$
Q2=\frac{40+50}{2}=45
$$

$$
Q3=\frac{60+70}{2}=65
$$

**Note:** Quartile calculation conventions vary. Pandas may produce slightly different values because of interpolation.

---

## 10. Interquartile Range (IQR)

IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of observations.

$$
IQR=Q3-Q1
$$

Example:

$$
IQR=65-25=40
$$

### Outlier detection using IQR

Lower fence:

$$
Q1-1.5(IQR)
$$

Upper fence:

$$
Q3+1.5(IQR)
$$

For the example:

Lower fence = `-35`

Upper fence = `125`

An observation of `150` exceeds the upper fence and is flagged as a potential outlier.

**Production rule:** Do not automatically remove an observation because it is statistically unusual. Investigate whether it represents legitimate behavior or a data-quality error.

---

## 11. Percentiles

Percentiles describe positions within an ordered distribution.

| Percentile | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| P25 | Approximately 25% of observations are at or below this value |
| P50 | Median |
| P75 | Approximately 75% are at or below |
| P90 | Approximately 90% are at or below |
| P95 | Approximately 95% are at or below |
| P99 | Approximately 99% are at or below |

Example:

If P95 watch time = 200 minutes, approximately 95% of observations have watch times of 200 minutes or less.

### Nearest-rank calculation

$$
Rank=\lceil p\times n\rceil
$$

Here, p is the percentile expressed as a fraction.

For 10 ordered observations:

$$
Rank(P90)=\lceil0.9\times10\rceil=9
$$

Select the ninth value.

**Important:** Different quantile calculation methods can return different answers, especially with small datasets.

### Production applications

Percentiles are frequently used to monitor:

- API latency
- Response times
- User watch time
- Transaction values
- Processing duration
- Extreme resource usage

P95 and P99 are particularly useful for investigating tail performance that averages may conceal.

---

## 12. Skewness

Skewness describes the asymmetry of a distribution.

### Positive Skewness

- Long right tail.
- Extreme values occur toward the higher end.
- Mean is often greater than median.

Examples: Salaries, purchase values, some latency distributions.

### Negative Skewness

- Long left tail.
- Extreme values occur toward the lower end.
- Mean is often less than median.

Example: Scores on an easy examination where most students score highly.

### Symmetric Distribution

- Similar shape on both sides of the center.
- Mean and median coincide for an exactly symmetric distribution with a finite mean and unique median.

**Important:** Comparing mean and median provides clues about skewness, but does not establish its mathematical sign in every dataset.

Population skewness is related to the third standardized central moment:

$$
\gamma_1=E\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^3\right]
$$

---

## 13. Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the contribution of extreme standardized deviations to a distribution.

Population kurtosis:

$$
\beta_2=E\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^4\right]
$$

Excess kurtosis:

$$
Excess\ Kurtosis=\beta_2-3
$$

| Type | Excess Kurtosis |
|---|---|
| Leptokurtic | Greater than 0 |
| Mesokurtic | Equal to 0 |
| Platykurtic | Less than 0 |

The Normal distribution has kurtosis 3 and excess kurtosis 0.

**Key insight:** Two datasets can have the same mean and standard deviation while differing substantially in the contribution from extreme values.

Kurtosis should not be interpreted simply as the height of a distribution's peak.

---

## 14. Covariance

Covariance measures how two variables vary together.

Population covariance:

$$
Cov(X,Y)=\frac{\sum(x_i-\mu_X)(y_i-\mu_Y)}{N}
$$

Example:

| Watch time X | Likes Y |
|---:|---:|
| 10 | 1 |
| 20 | 2 |
| 30 | 3 |

Means:

X = 20  
Y = 2

Products of deviations:

`10, 0, 10`

$$
Cov(X,Y)=\frac{20}{3}=+6.67
$$

### Interpretation

- **Positive:** Variables tend to move in the same direction.
- **Negative:** Variables tend to move in opposite directions.
- **Zero:** No linear co-movement detected.

**Limitation:** Covariance depends on measurement units and feature scales.

For the usual sample covariance estimate, use `n − 1` in the denominator.

---

## 15. Pearson Correlation

Pearson correlation measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship.

$$
r=\frac{Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}
$$

### Interpretation

- +1: Perfect positive linear relationship.
- 0: No linear association.
- -1: Perfect negative linear relationship.

**Why Pearson instead of covariance?**

Dividing covariance by the standard deviations standardizes it, removing measurement units.

### Important properties

- Ranges from -1 to +1.
- Measures linear association.
- Sensitive to numerical outliers.
- Correlation does not imply causation.
- Zero Pearson correlation does not necessarily imply independence.

Pearson correlation is undefined if either variable has zero standard deviation.

---

## 16. Spearman Correlation

Spearman correlation measures monotonic association using ranks.

Unlike Pearson, it does not require a straight-line relationship.

Example:

| X | Y |
|---|---|
| 1 | 10 |
| 2 | 20 |
| 3 | 30 |
| 4 | 40 |
| 5 | 100 |

Although Y suddenly increases substantially, the ranking of observations is perfectly preserved.

Spearman correlation = +1.

Pearson correlation is approximately +0.894.

### Pearson vs Spearman

| Pearson | Spearman |
|---|---|
| Actual numerical values | Ranked values |
| Linear association | Monotonic association |
| Sensitive to extreme magnitudes | Less affected by extreme magnitudes |
| Perfect +1 requires an increasing straight-line relationship | Perfect +1 requires identical rankings |

**Use Spearman** when rank order matters or when evaluating a monotonic but nonlinear relationship.

---

## 17. Z-Score

Z-score represents the number of standard deviations an observation lies above or below the mean.

$$
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
$$

Example:

Mean API latency = 100 ms  
Standard deviation = 20 ms  
Observed latency = 180 ms

$$
z=\frac{180-100}{20}=+4
$$

The observation is four standard deviations above the mean.

### Interpretation

- Z = 0: Equal to mean.
- Z = +2: Two standard deviations above mean.
- Z = -2: Two standard deviations below mean.

### Outlier detection

A commonly used heuristic:

$$
|z|>3
$$

Under a Normal distribution, approximately 99.73% of observations lie within three standard deviations of the mean.

**Limitations:**

- Z-score thresholds are not universally reliable.
- Heavily skewed distributions can produce misleading flags.
- Mean and standard deviation are themselves sensitive to extremes.

### ML standardization

Z-score standardization is also used to put numerical features on comparable scales.

To avoid data leakage, calculate the mean and standard deviation from the training data only, then apply the transformation to validation and test data.

## 18. Correlation Pitfalls and Nonlinear Relationships

Consider:

| Notifications X | Satisfaction Y |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 1 |

Satisfaction initially increases, then decreases.

This is an inverted U-shaped relationship.

Despite the clear dependence between X and Y:

**Pearson correlation = 0.**

Why?

Because positive and negative contributions to covariance cancel.

### Machine Learning implications

Never immediately discard a feature simply because it has low Pearson correlation with the target.

A feature might have:

- A nonlinear relationship.
- A U-shaped relationship.
- An interaction with another feature.
- Predictive value within specific population segments.

Use visualizations and appropriate out-of-sample validation.

Decision Trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting models, and neural networks can learn various nonlinear patterns.

**Correlation does not prove causation.** Confounding variables and selection bias can create misleading associations.

## 19. Production Data Science — Google-Scale Considerations

When analyzing millions or billions of records, consider:

1. **Correct metric definitions:** Per event, per session, per user, and per user-day statistics mean different things.
2. **Data quality:** Duplicate records, missing values, invalid timestamps, instrumentation errors.
3. **Sampling:** Samples should represent the target population.
4. **Dependence:** Multiple events from the same user should not automatically be treated as independent users.
5. **Distributed aggregation:** Use partition-level sums and counts for mergeable statistics.
6. **Quantile estimation:** Approximate sketches such as KLL or t-digest can help compute percentiles efficiently at scale.
7. **Outliers:** Investigate before removing or capping observations.
8. **Distribution shifts:** Metrics can change because the mix of users changes.
9. **Data leakage:** Fit preprocessing statistics on training data only.
10. **Causal reasoning:** Association alone is insufficient for claiming that a product change caused an outcome.

## 20. Interview Revision

**Q1. Why is median preferred for highly skewed data?**

Because it is less sensitive to extreme values than mean.

**Q2. Can two datasets have identical means and different variances?**

Yes. Mean measures center; variance measures spread.

**Q3. Why divide sample variance by n − 1?**

To correct the usual downward bias when estimating population variance using deviations from the sample mean.

**Q4. Is every IQR outlier invalid?**

No. Unusual observations can represent legitimate activity.

**Q5. What does P99 latency mean?**

Approximately 99% of observed requests have latency at or below the P99 threshold.

**Q6. What does high positive skewness suggest?**

A distribution with a longer right tail.

**Q7. What is the difference between covariance and correlation?**

Covariance is scale-dependent; correlation standardizes the association.

**Q8. When can Spearman be +1 while Pearson is below +1?**

When variables maintain perfect rank order but don't form a perfect straight line.

**Q9. Can Pearson correlation be zero for dependent variables?**

Yes. For example, a symmetric U-shaped relationship.

**Q10. What's the difference between standard deviation and Z-score?**

Standard deviation describes overall spread; Z-score measures an individual observation's standardized distance from the mean.

**Q11. Why shouldn't we simply average shard-level P99 values?**

Because the average of partition percentiles is generally not the global percentile.

**Q12. What is the first thing to establish when calculating statistics from billions of events?*

The population, metric definition, and correct unit of analysis.
