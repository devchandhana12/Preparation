
## 11.8 Optional Stopping and Repeated Peeking

Imagine we're conducting a YouTube A/B experiment.

The original plan is to run it for 14 days.

Our manager asks us to check the p-value every day.

Suppose the results look like this:

| Day | Cumulative p-value |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 0.32 |
| 2 | 0.18 |
| 3 | 0.08 |
| 4 | 0.04 |
| 5 | 0.09 |
| 6 | 0.12 |

On Day 4, the p-value becomes:

$$
p=0.04
$$

The manager says:

> Stop the experiment! We've achieved statistical significance.

But there's a problem.

We originally designed the experiment to use a conventional fixed-sample hypothesis test.

Repeatedly checking its accumulating p-value and stopping the first time it becomes significant can increase the false-positive probability beyond our intended alpha.

This is known as **Optional Stopping**, and the repeated inspection is often called **Peeking**.

### Why Is This Problematic?

A conventional fixed-sample test assumes a specified analysis and stopping procedure.

If we repeatedly check for significance, we create additional opportunities to stop on a favorable random fluctuation.

The sequence of daily results also shares accumulating observations, so those checks are dependent.

We cannot calculate the exact error inflation merely by treating the daily tests as independent.

### Correct Production Approaches

**Approach 1 — Fixed-sample Testing**

Before the experiment:

- Choose the required sample size.
- Define the primary metric.
- Define the testing method.
- Set the significance level.
- Define the experiment duration and stopping conditions.

Perform the confirmatory test according to that plan.

**Approach 2 — Group Sequential Testing**

Plan multiple interim analyses and adjust the statistical rejection boundaries to control overall Type I error.

**Approach 3 — Alpha Spending**

Allocate a controlled Type I error budget across planned interim analyses.

**Approach 4 — Always-valid Sequential Methods**

Use appropriately designed sequential testing procedures that permit monitoring while maintaining their stated guarantees.

### Important

Monitoring an experiment isn't inherently wrong.

The problem arises when a statistical decision procedure does not account for the way results are repeatedly examined or used for stopping.

## 11.9 Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)

Imagine we randomly assign YouTube users equally between two algorithms.

Expected allocation:

| Group | Expected Users |
|---|---:|
| Algorithm A | 50,000 |
| Algorithm B | 50,000 |

But our observed experiment data contains:

| Group | Observed Users |
|---|---:|
| Algorithm A | 60,000 |
| Algorithm B | 40,000 |

This is a substantial deviation from the planned 50:50 assignment.

The problem is called **Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)** when observed assignment or analysis counts deviate unexpectedly from the planned allocation.

### What Could Cause SRM?

Potential causes include:

- Incorrect randomization logic.
- Tracking or logging failures.
- Treatment-specific data loss.
- Incorrect experiment eligibility rules.
- Assignment inconsistencies.
- Filtering applied differently across groups.

### Why Does SRM Matter?

If the randomization or observation process is compromised, the two groups may not be comparable as intended.

The observed treatment effect could be biased.

### Production Response

Before trusting the experiment:

1. Compare observed allocation with the planned ratio.
2. Investigate statistically unusual discrepancies.
3. Inspect assignment and logging pipelines.
4. Identify treatment-specific data loss.
5. Fix the issue and determine whether the experiment remains valid.

### Interview Question

**Q: Your experiment has a statistically significant result, but you detect severe SRM. Would you deploy?**

**Answer:**

Not solely based on that result.

I would investigate SRM because it may indicate a randomization, logging, eligibility, or data-quality problem.

Until the issue is understood, the experiment's causal interpretation may be unreliable.

## 11.10 Unit of Randomization vs Unit of Analysis

This is particularly important at large-scale technology companies.

Imagine YouTube randomly assigns users to algorithms.

Each user may watch hundreds of videos.

Suppose our dataset contains:

- 10,000 randomized users.
- 1,000,000 video-view events.

Can we treat all one million events as independent observations?

**Not automatically.**

Events generated by the same user may be correlated.

### Randomization Unit

The unit assigned to an experimental treatment.

In our experiment:

**User**

### Analysis Unit

The unit used to construct the outcome or statistical comparison.

For example:

**User-level average daily watch time**

### Why Does This Matter?

If we pretend correlated events are independent, we may underestimate uncertainty.

That can produce misleadingly small Standard Errors and p-values.

### Possible Solutions

Depending on the experimental design:

- Aggregate observations at the user level.
- Use cluster-robust Standard Errors.
- Use appropriate hierarchical models.
- Use cluster-based resampling methods.

### Interview Takeaway

**One million event rows do not necessarily represent one million independent experimental units.**

## 11.11 Interference Between Users

A common simplifying assumption in randomized experiments is that one user's treatment doesn't affect another user's outcome.

But this assumption can fail.

Imagine YouTube tests a new sharing feature.

A user assigned to Algorithm B shares a video with a friend assigned to Algorithm A.

The friend watches more videos because of that share.

Now one user's treatment has affected another user's outcome.

This is called **Interference** or a spillover effect.

### Why Is This Important?

Ordinary user-level A/B testing may no longer estimate the intended direct causal effect cleanly.

### Production Considerations

Depending on the product:

- Randomize at a larger cluster level.
- Consider network relationships.
- Use experimental designs that account for spillovers.
- Interpret the estimated effect according to the actual design.

Interference matters in:

- Social networks.
- Recommendation systems.
- Messaging products.
- Marketplaces.
- Collaborative software.

## 11.12 Novelty Effects and Seasonality

Suppose Algorithm B is launched just before a major festival or sporting event.

Average watch time increases significantly.

Does that prove the new algorithm caused the increase?

Not necessarily.

If A and B were randomly assigned and measured concurrently, a shared festival effect is generally balanced between groups on average.

However, time effects can still matter when:

- Treatments run during different periods.
- User composition changes over time.
- There are interactions between treatment and season.
- The experiment is too short to capture typical usage.
- Early excitement fades as users become accustomed to the change.

### Novelty Effect

Users may initially respond strongly to a new feature simply because it's new.

That effect may weaken over time.

### Seasonality

Behavior may change with:

- Weekends.
- Holidays.
- Sporting events.
- Academic calendars.
- Seasonal trends.

### Production Takeaway

An experiment should run long enough and use an appropriate design to support the intended deployment decision.

## 11.13 Practical Significance vs Statistical Significance

Imagine YouTube's new algorithm improves average watch time by:

$$
0.01\text{ minutes}
$$

With tens of millions of observations, the p-value could be extremely small.

But is an improvement of 0.01 minutes valuable?

That is a business and product question.

Now consider another algorithm improving watch time by:

$$
3\text{ minutes}
$$

But the experiment is underpowered and does not reach statistical significance.

The second effect estimate is larger, but the statistical uncertainty is greater.

### Statistical Significance

Is the observed effect sufficiently inconsistent with the null hypothesis under the chosen testing procedure?

### Practical Significance

Is the effect large enough to matter for the business, product, or users?

### Production Decision

Consider both.

Statistical significance alone does not tell us whether an improvement justifies deployment.

# Formula Cheat Sheet

## 1. Treatment Effect

$$
\Delta=\mu_B-\mu_A
$$

Observed estimate:

$$
\hat{\Delta}=\bar{x}_B-\bar{x}_A
$$

---

## 2. One-sided Improvement Hypotheses

$$
H_0:\Delta\leq0
$$

$$
H_1:\Delta>0
$$

---

## 3. Two-sided Hypotheses

$$
H_0:\Delta=0
$$

$$
H_1:\Delta\neq0
$$

---

## 4. Standardized Test Statistic

$$
Z=
\frac{\hat{\Delta}-\Delta_0}
{SE_{\text{difference}}}
$$

---

## 5. SE for Independent Group Means

When population variances are known:

$$
SE_{\text{difference}}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{\sigma_A^2}{n_A}
+
\frac{\sigma_B^2}{n_B}
}
$$

Estimated using sample variances:

$$
\widehat{SE}_{\text{difference}}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}
+
\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}
}
$$

---

## 6. Welch's Two-sample t-statistic

$$
t=
\frac{
(\bar{x}_B-\bar{x}_A)-\Delta_0
}{
\sqrt{
\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}
+
\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}
}
}
$$

---

## 7. P-value — Upper-tailed Z-test

$$
p=P(Z\geq Z_{\text{observed}}\mid H_0)
$$

---

## 8. P-value — Two-tailed Z-test

For a Standard Normal null distribution:

$$
p=2P(Z\geq|Z_{\text{observed}}|)
$$

---

## 9. Statistical Decision Rule

$$
p\leq\alpha
\Rightarrow
\text{Reject }H_0
$$

$$
p>\alpha
\Rightarrow
\text{Fail to reject }H_0
$$

---

## 10. Statistical Power

$$
\boxed{\text{Power}=1-\beta}
$$

---

## 11. Approximate Confidence Interval for Mean Difference

For an appropriate large-sample Normal approximation:

$$
CI=
\hat{\Delta}\pm
Z_{\text{critical}}
\times
\widehat{SE}_{\text{difference}}
$$

For a 95% two-sided interval:

$$
Z_{\text{critical}}\approx1.96
$$

For a t-based interval, replace the Normal critical value with the appropriate t critical value.

---

## 12. Approximate Sample Size for A/B Testing

For equal-sized independent groups, approximately equal population variances, and a two-sided Normal-based test:

$$
n_{\text{per group}}
\approx
\frac{
2\sigma^2
(Z_{1-\alpha/2}+Z_{1-\beta})^2
}{
\delta^2
}
$$

Here:

- sigma = Assumed population SD.
- delta = Effect we want to detect.
- alpha = Significance level.
- beta = Type II error probability.

---

## 13. Family-wise False-positive Risk

For m independent tests, all null hypotheses true, and false-positive probability alpha per test:

$$
FWER=1-(1-\alpha)^m
$$

---

## 14. Bonferroni Correction

$$
\alpha_{\text{adjusted}}=\frac{\alpha}{m}
$$

---

## 15. Common Standard Normal Critical Values

| Test | Alpha | Critical Value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Right-tailed | 0.05 | +1.645 |
| Left-tailed | 0.05 | -1.645 |
| Two-tailed | 0.05 | ±1.960 |
| Two-tailed | 0.01 | ±2.576 |

# Production Interview Scenarios

## Scenario 1 — Statistically Significant but Tiny Improvement

**Interviewer:**

> Your recommendation algorithm improves average watch time by 0.01 minutes, with p = 0.0001. Would you deploy?

**Strong Answer:**

Not automatically.

The result is statistically significant, but we need to evaluate practical significance.

I would examine the confidence interval, cost of deploying the algorithm, effects on user experience, guardrail metrics, and expected business impact.

A small p-value does not imply a large effect.

---

## Scenario 2 — Large Improvement but Nonsignificant Result

**Interviewer:**

> Algorithm B improves sample mean watch time by 5 minutes, but p = 0.12. What would you conclude?

**Strong Answer:**

The experiment does not provide sufficient evidence to reject H0 at alpha = 0.05.

However, that doesn't prove there is no improvement.

I would examine the effect estimate, confidence interval, sample size, variability, and whether the experiment was adequately powered.

The next step depends on how uncertain the estimate is and whether further testing is worthwhile.

---

## Scenario 3 — Increasing Sample Size

**Interviewer:**

> Why does a larger sample generally improve statistical power?

**Strong Answer:**

Increasing the number of independent observations usually reduces the Standard Error.

For a fixed true effect, that makes the estimated effect easier to distinguish from random variability, increasing the probability of rejecting a false null hypothesis.

The benefit depends on the sampling design and effective independence of observations.

---

## Scenario 4 — Five Metrics Are Significant

**Interviewer:**

> You test 100 product metrics and find five with p-values below 0.05. Have you discovered five improvements?

**Strong Answer:**

Not necessarily.

Testing many hypotheses increases opportunities for false-positive findings.

I would examine the prespecified primary metric, the family of confirmatory tests, and whether appropriate multiplicity adjustments were used.

Exploratory findings may require confirmation in a new experiment.

---

## Scenario 5 — Repeated Peeking

**Interviewer:**

> The PM checks p-values hourly and stops as soon as p drops below 0.05. What's the issue?

**Strong Answer:**

An ordinary fixed-sample test may not maintain its nominal false-positive rate under repeated significance-based stopping.

I would use a prespecified sample size and stopping plan, or an appropriate sequential-testing method.

---

## Scenario 6 — Sample Ratio Mismatch

**Interviewer:**

> Your experiment was planned for 50:50 allocation but resulted in 65:35. What would you investigate?

**Strong Answer:**

I would examine the randomization service, eligibility filters, event logging, treatment exposure, and treatment-specific missing data.

A severe mismatch could indicate a problem affecting the experiment's validity.

I would investigate it before relying on the treatment-effect estimate.

---

## Scenario 7 — One Million Events but Only 5,000 Users

**Interviewer:**

> Can you treat one million video-view events as one million independent observations?

**Strong Answer:**

Not automatically.

Events from the same user may be correlated.

If randomization occurs at the user level, I would generally consider user-level aggregation or an inference method accounting for within-user dependence.

Ignoring correlation can underestimate uncertainty.

---

## Scenario 8 — Choosing Between Z-test and t-test

**Interviewer:**

> When would you use a Z-test versus a t-test for comparing means?

**Strong Answer:**

A classical Z-test uses a Normal reference distribution and may be appropriate when relevant population variances are known or a justified large-sample Normal approximation is used.

When variances are unknown and estimated from the samples, a t-test is often suitable.

For two independent groups with potentially unequal variances, Welch's two-sample t-test is a common choice.

The decision depends on assumptions and experimental design, not only whether sample size is above or below 30.

---

## Scenario 9 — Confidence Interval Includes Zero

**Interviewer:**

> An estimated watch-time increase is 2 minutes with a 95% CI of [-1, 5]. What does it tell you?

**Strong Answer:**

The estimate is positive, but the interval includes zero and negative effects.

For a matching two-sided 5% hypothesis test, we would generally fail to reject the no-difference null hypothesis.

The interval indicates that the data are compatible with a range of population effects under the model.

I would not claim the algorithm is proven ineffective or beneficial.

---

## Scenario 10 — Algorithm Improves Watch Time but Increases Crashes

**Interviewer:**

> Algorithm B significantly increases watch time but also increases app crashes. Should you deploy?

**Strong Answer:**

Not solely based on watch time.

I would evaluate whether the change meets predefined guardrail criteria, consider user experience, business impact, and operational risk.

Statistical significance of one primary metric does not override unacceptable adverse outcomes.

# Common Interview Traps

## Trap 1 — P-value Equals Probability H0 Is True

Incorrect.

A conventional p-value measures the extremeness of the observed test statistic under the null model.

It does not directly calculate the posterior probability that H0 is true.

---

## Trap 2 — Small P-value Means Large Effect

Incorrect.

A small effect can have a very small p-value when estimated precisely.

Always report effect size and uncertainty.

---

## Trap 3 — Failing to Reject H0 Proves No Effect

Incorrect.

Failure to reject means insufficient evidence under the chosen test.

It does not establish equivalence or absence of an effect.

---

## Trap 4 — Alpha and Beta Are the Same

Incorrect.

Alpha concerns false rejection under H0.

Beta concerns missed detection under a specified alternative.

---

## Trap 5 — Power Is the Probability H1 Is True

Incorrect.

Power is the probability of rejecting H0 given a specified genuine effect and the test design.

---

## Trap 6 — Larger Samples Automatically Remove Bias

Incorrect.

Larger samples generally reduce random error but do not automatically correct selection bias, measurement bias, or invalid experimental design.

---

## Trap 7 — One-tailed Tests Are Always Better

Incorrect.

One-tailed tests are appropriate for prespecified directional alternatives.

Two-tailed tests are appropriate when meaningful effects can occur in either direction.

---

## Trap 8 — Use t-test Only When n Is Below 30

Incorrect.

This is an oversimplification.

The choice depends on variability estimation, distributional assumptions, independence, and study design.

---

## Trap 9 — Statistical Significance Guarantees Deployment

Incorrect.

Business impact, guardrail metrics, data quality, uncertainty, and rollout risk matter.

---

## Trap 10 — A/B Testing Is Just Comparing Two Means

Incorrect.

A rigorous A/B experiment includes randomization, metric definition, sample-size planning, experimental validity checks, appropriate statistical analysis, and decision-making.

---

## Trap 11 — Checking P-values Repeatedly Is Harmless

Incorrect.

Unplanned repeated significance-based stopping can inflate the false-positive rate.

Use a suitable fixed-sample or sequential-testing plan.

---

## Trap 12 — More Tested Metrics Means More Genuine Discoveries

Not necessarily.

Testing more metrics creates more opportunities for false discoveries.

Confirmatory analysis should account for multiple testing.

# Rapid Interview Revision

## Question: What Is Hypothesis Testing?

A method for evaluating population-level statistical claims using sample evidence under specified assumptions.

## Question: What Is H0?

The null hypothesis, used as the reference claim for the test.

## Question: What Is H1?

The alternative hypothesis, representing the effect or difference we're examining.

## Question: What Is Alpha?

The maximum Type I error probability permitted by the chosen valid test.

## Question: What Is a Test Statistic?

A number calculated from sample data whose null distribution helps assess evidence against H0.

## Question: What Is a P-value?

The probability, under the null model, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one calculated.

## Question: What Is a Type I Error?

Rejecting a true null hypothesis.

## Question: What Is a Type II Error?

Failing to reject a false null hypothesis.

## Question: What Is Statistical Power?

The probability of rejecting H0 under a specified genuine effect.

## Question: What Is the Power Formula?

Power = 1 - Beta.

## Question: What Increases Statistical Power?

Generally, larger independent samples, larger effects in the tested direction, and lower variability.

## Question: What Is a One-tailed Test?

A test with an alternative hypothesis in one prespecified direction.

## Question: What Is a Two-tailed Test?

A test that examines departures from the null in either direction.

## Question: Why Use Welch's t-test?

To compare independent group means when population variances are unknown and may differ.

## Question: What Is Multiple Testing?

Conducting several hypothesis tests, which creates additional opportunities for false discoveries.

## Question: What Is Bonferroni Correction?

A method that compares each p-value with alpha divided by the number of tests to control family-wise error.

## Question: What Is Optional Stopping?

Allowing the statistical stopping decision to depend on accumulating results, which requires an appropriate testing procedure to preserve error guarantees.

## Question: What Is SRM?

An unexpected mismatch between the planned and observed experiment allocation ratios.

## Question: What Are Guardrail Metrics?

Additional product or system measurements used to identify unacceptable side effects of a treatment.

## Question: What Is the Difference Between Statistical and Practical Significance?

Statistical significance concerns evidence against a null hypothesis.

Practical significance concerns whether the effect is large and valuable enough to matter.
