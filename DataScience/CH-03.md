# Lesson 01 — Random Variables

## 1.1 Why do we need random variables?

Imagine we're building YouTube's recommendation system.

We recommend one video to a user.

There are two possible outcomes:

1. The user clicks the video.
2. The user does not click the video.

Before the recommendation is shown, we don't know which outcome will happen.

This makes it a random experiment.

The set of possible outcomes is called the sample space.

$$
S = \{\text{Click}, \text{No Click}\}
$$

However, mathematical and statistical models usually work with numbers.

We therefore define:

$$
X =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if the user clicks} \\
0 & \text{if the user does not click}
\end{cases}
$$

Here, X is our random variable.

### Definition

A random variable is a function that assigns a numerical value to each possible outcome of a random experiment.

**Important:** A random variable is not necessarily unpredictable forever. It represents a quantity whose value is uncertain before the relevant observation.

## 1.2 Understanding the notation

Consider:

$$
P(X=1)=0.30
$$

This means:

- X is the random variable.
- 1 is a possible value.
- P represents probability.
- 0.30 means a 30% probability of clicking.

Therefore:

$$
P(X=0)=0.70
$$

A random variable tells us what we're measuring.

A probability distribution tells us how likely each possible value is.

These are different concepts.

## 1.3 Example: Multiple recommendations

Suppose we recommend three videos.

Define:

$$
X = \text{Number of videos clicked}
$$

Possible values:

$$
X \in \{0,1,2,3\}
$$

A user may click none, one, two, or all three videos.

The random variable describes the number of clicks, but does not yet tell us the probability of each count.

That is where probability distributions become useful.

### Interview takeaway

**Q: What is the difference between a random variable and a probability distribution?**

A random variable assigns numbers to uncertain outcomes. A probability distribution specifies how probability is assigned to its possible values.

# Lesson 02 — Discrete vs Continuous Random Variables

## 2.1 Discrete Random Variables

A discrete random variable takes values from a finite or countably infinite set.

Think of counting something.

### Example: YouTube clicks

Suppose we recommend three videos.

$$
X = \text{Number of videos clicked}
$$

Possible values:

$$
X \in \{0,1,2,3\}
$$

Can someone click exactly 1.75 videos?

No.

Clicks are counted as whole numbers.

Therefore, X is discrete.

### Additional examples

- Number of purchases by a customer
- Number of API requests per second
- Number of defective products
- Number of successful login attempts
- Number of failed transactions

Discrete variables can have infinitely many possible values, as long as the values are countable.

For example, a Poisson-distributed request count can take:

$$
0,1,2,3,\ldots
$$

## 2.2 Continuous Random Variables

A continuous random variable can take values anywhere along a continuous interval.

Think of measuring something.

### Example: Video watch time

Let:

$$
T = \text{Time spent watching a video}
$$

Possible values include:

- 2 minutes
- 2.5 minutes
- 2.75 minutes
- 2.751 minutes
- 2.75193 minutes

A continuous mathematical model allows infinitely many values between two different points.

Therefore, T is continuous.

### Additional examples

- Temperature
- Height
- Weight
- Network latency
- Delivery time
- Waiting time until the next API request

### Important production distinction

Just because a database stores a value as an integer doesn't automatically mean the underlying quantity should be modeled as discrete.

For example, latency may be stored as 175 milliseconds, even though the actual elapsed time is continuous.

Storage precision and mathematical modeling are different decisions.

## 2.3 Why does this distinction matter?

For discrete variables, we can directly calculate the probability of an exact outcome.

Example:

$$
P(X=2)
$$

This asks for the probability of exactly two clicks.

For continuous variables, we usually calculate probabilities over intervals.

Example:

$$
P(2 \lt T \lt 5)
$$

This asks for the probability of watch time between 2 and 5 minutes.

For a continuous random variable:

$$
P(T=3)=0
$$

We'll understand why in the PDF lesson.

### Interview takeaway

**Q: Is API latency discrete or continuous?**

Typically continuous, because elapsed time is a measured quantity, even if the monitoring system rounds it to milliseconds.

# Lesson 03 — Probability Mass Function (PMF)

## 3.1 Why do we need PMF?

Suppose YouTube recommends two videos.

Define:

$$
X = \text{Number of videos clicked}
$$

Possible values:

$$
X \in \{0,1,2\}
$$

But the possible values alone are not enough.

Our manager wants to know:

"What is the probability of exactly one click?"

To answer that, we need probabilities associated with each possible count.

That's what a Probability Mass Function provides.

## 3.2 Let's build a PMF manually

Assume:

- Each video has a 50% probability of being clicked.
- Click decisions are independent.

These are simplifying assumptions for this example.

Represent:

- C = Click
- N = No Click

The possible outcomes are:

| Video 1 | Video 2 | Total Clicks |
|---|---|---|
| N | N | 0 |
| C | N | 1 |
| N | C | 1 |
| C | C | 2 |

Each sequence has probability:

$$
0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25
$$

### Probability of zero clicks

Only NN produces zero clicks.

$$
P(X=0)=0.25
$$

### Probability of exactly one click

Two sequences produce exactly one click:

- CN
- NC

Therefore:

$$
P(X=1)=0.25+0.25=0.50
$$

### Probability of exactly two clicks

Only CC produces two clicks.

$$
P(X=2)=0.25
$$

Our complete distribution is:

| X | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.25 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.25 |

This table is our PMF.

## 3.3 PMF definition

A Probability Mass Function specifies the probability of each possible value of a discrete random variable.

$$
p_X(x)=P(X=x)
$$

Here:

- X is the random variable.
- x represents a particular possible value.
- p_X(x) gives the probability associated with x.

## 3.4 Properties of PMF

### Property 1: Probability cannot be negative

$$
P(X=x)\geq0
$$

### Property 2: Total probability must equal 1

$$
\sum_x P(X=x)=1
$$

Check our example:

$$
0.25+0.50+0.25=1
$$

This works because we've included every possible outcome.

## 3.5 Calculating probabilities involving multiple outcomes

### Example: At least one click

"At least one" means one or two clicks.

$$
P(X\geq1)=P(X=1)+P(X=2)
$$

$$
=0.50+0.25
$$

$$
=0.75
$$

Therefore, the probability is 75%.

### Alternative: Use the complement

The opposite of "at least one click" is "zero clicks."

$$
P(X\geq1)=1-P(X=0)
$$

$$
=1-0.25=0.75
$$

Both approaches give the same result.

## 3.6 Production considerations

In real recommendation systems, click probabilities depend on:

- User preferences
- Recommendation ranking
- Video topic
- Device
- Time of day
- Previous user interactions

Therefore, we would estimate a PMF from relevant observed data instead of assuming every click has a 50% probability.

We must also define the observation window correctly.

"Clicks across two impressions" and "clicks in one day" are different random variables.

### Interview question

A PMF has:

$$
P(X=0)=0.20
$$

$$
P(X=1)=0.50
$$

X can only take values 0, 1, and 2.

Find P(X=2).

**Solution:**

$$
P(X=2)=1-(0.20+0.50)
$$

$$
P(X=2)=0.30
$$

# Lesson 04 — Probability Density Function (PDF)

## 4.1 Why can't we always use PMF?

PMF works for discrete random variables.

But suppose we're measuring video watch time.

Define:

$$
T = \text{Watch time in minutes}
$$

T is continuous.

Our manager asks:

"What's the probability that someone watches between 3 and 5 minutes?"

There are infinitely many possible watch times within that interval.

Rather than assigning a positive probability to each exact value, continuous distributions describe probability through density.

That is what the Probability Density Function does.

## 4.2 Understanding density

For simplicity, suppose watch time is uniformly distributed between 0 and 10 minutes.

This means equal-length intervals have equal probability.

The PDF is:

$$
f_T(t)=
\begin{cases}
0.1 & 0\leq t\leq10 \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

Why is the density 0.1?

Because the total area under a PDF must equal 1.

Our density graph is a rectangle.

Rectangle width:

$$
10-0=10
$$

Rectangle height:

$$
0.1
$$

Area:

$$
10\times0.1=1
$$

## 4.3 Calculating probability using area

Question:

What's the probability of watching between 3 and 5 minutes?

For a rectangle:

$$
\text{Area}=\text{Width}\times\text{Height}
$$

Width:

$$
5-3=2
$$

Height:

$$
0.1
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(3\leq T\leq5)=2\times0.1
$$

$$
=0.20
$$

The probability is 20%.

## 4.4 General PDF formula

For a continuous random variable:

$$
P(a\leq T\leq b)=\int_a^b f_T(t)\,dt
$$

The integral represents the area under the density curve between a and b.

For our rectangular distribution, this is simply width multiplied by height.

For curved distributions, integration gives the area.

## 4.5 Why is probability at an exact point zero?

Suppose we ask:

"What is the probability of exactly 5 minutes of watch time?"

For a continuous random variable:

$$
P(T=5)=0
$$

This can seem strange.

The reasoning is that one exact point has zero width.

Therefore, the area associated with that point is zero.

However, that doesn't mean a measurement device cannot display 5.00 minutes.

A displayed measurement has limited precision and typically represents a small interval.

### Crucial distinction

$$
f_T(5)=0.1
$$

does not mean:

$$
P(T=5)=0.1
$$

The first is density.

The second is the probability of an exact point.

These are different.

## 4.6 Properties of PDF

### Property 1: Density is nonnegative

$$
f_T(t)\geq0
$$

### Property 2: Total area equals 1

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_T(t)\,dt=1
$$

### Property 3: PDF height may exceed 1

This is an important interview trap.

Suppose a uniform random variable lies between 0 and 0.2.

Its PDF height is:

$$
\frac{1}{0.2}=5
$$

The height is 5, but the total area is:

$$
5\times0.2=1
$$

So a PDF value greater than 1 is valid.

Probability itself must remain between 0 and 1.

## 4.7 PMF vs PDF

| PMF | PDF |
|---|---|
| Discrete variables | Continuous variables |
| Exact-value probability | Probability density |
| Sum probabilities | Integrate density |
| Individual values may have positive probability | Individual exact points have zero probability |
| Total probability sums to 1 | Total area equals 1 |

### Production example

Suppose our API latency is continuous.

We might calculate:

- Probability latency exceeds 500 ms
- Probability latency falls between 100 and 200 ms
- Probability latency remains below an SLO threshold

These are interval and tail probabilities.

### Interview question

**Q: Can a PDF value be greater than 1?**

Yes. PDF height is density, not probability. The requirement is that total area under the density is 1.

# Lesson 05 — Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

## 5.1 Why do we need CDF?

Suppose we know the watch-time PDF.

Now our manager asks:

"What is the probability that a user watches for 4 minutes or less?"

We want to accumulate all probability up to 4 minutes.

That's what the Cumulative Distribution Function does.

## 5.2 Definition

The CDF of a random variable X is:

$$
F_X(x)=P(X\leq x)
$$

Read this as:

"The probability that X is less than or equal to x."

CDF works for both discrete and continuous random variables.

## 5.3 Continuous example

Our watch time is uniformly distributed between 0 and 10 minutes.

The PDF height is 0.1.

Find:

$$
P(T\leq4)
$$

We need the area from 0 to 4.

Width:

$$
4-0=4
$$

Height:

$$
0.1
$$

Therefore:

$$
F_T(4)=4\times0.1=0.40
$$

So 40% of possible watch-time observations lie at or below 4 minutes under this model.

Similarly:

$$
F_T(8)=8\times0.1=0.80
$$

## 5.4 Probability between two thresholds

Our manager asks:

"What's the probability of watching between 4 and 8 minutes?"

We know:

$$
F_T(4)=0.40
$$

$$
F_T(8)=0.80
$$

The probability accumulated between these points is:

$$
P(4\lt T\leq8)=F_T(8)-F_T(4)
$$

$$
=0.80-0.40
$$

$$
=0.40
$$

So the probability is 40%.

### General CDF difference formula

$$
P(a\lt X\leq b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a)
$$

**Interpretation:**

- F_X(b) includes everything at or below b.
- F_X(a) includes everything at or below a.
- Subtracting removes the probability at or below a.
- The remainder is probability greater than a and at or below b.

This formula works for discrete and continuous distributions.

## 5.5 Discrete CDF example

Return to our two-video click PMF.

| Number of clicks | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.25 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.25 |

Calculate the CDF.

### At X = 0

$$
F_X(0)=P(X\leq0)=0.25
$$

### At X = 1

$$
F_X(1)=P(X\leq1)
$$

$$
=0.25+0.50=0.75
$$

### At X = 2

$$
F_X(2)=P(X\leq2)
$$

$$
=0.25+0.50+0.25=1
$$

Complete table:

| X | PMF | CDF |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.25 | 0.25 |
| 1 | 0.50 | 0.75 |
| 2 | 0.25 | 1.00 |

The discrete CDF increases in steps.

## 5.6 Important endpoint distinction

For continuous variables, the probability of an exact point is zero.

Therefore:

$$
P(a\lt X\leq b)=P(a\leq X\leq b)
$$

For discrete variables, this may not be true.

Using the clicks example:

$$
P(1\lt X\leq2)=0.25
$$

But:

$$
P(1\leq X\leq2)=0.50+0.25
$$

$$
=0.75
$$

The difference occurs because X = 1 has positive probability.

## 5.7 Properties of CDF

1. A CDF is nondecreasing.
2. Its values lie between 0 and 1.
3. It approaches 0 toward negative infinity.
4. It approaches 1 toward positive infinity.
5. It is right-continuous.

### PMF vs PDF vs CDF

| Function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| PMF | Exact probabilities for discrete values |
| PDF | Density for continuous variables |
| CDF | Accumulated probability up to a threshold |

### Interview question

Given:

$$
F_X(2)=0.25
$$

$$
F_X(5)=0.70
$$

Find:

$$
P(2\lt X\leq5)
$$

**Solution:**

$$
0.70-0.25=0.45
$$

# Lesson 06 — Expected Value and Variance

## 6.1 Why do we need Expected Value?

Suppose we know the click-count distribution:

| Clicks | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.25 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.25 |

Our manager asks:

"How many clicks should we expect on average?"

We don't know the outcome for an individual user.

But we know how likely each possible outcome is.

We can use a probability-weighted average.

This is Expected Value.

## 6.2 Expected Value formula

For a discrete random variable:

$$
E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x)
$$

This means:

Multiply every possible value by its probability, then add all the contributions.

## 6.3 Worked example

### Step 1: Zero clicks

$$
0\times0.25=0
$$

### Step 2: One click

$$
1\times0.50=0.50
$$

### Step 3: Two clicks

$$
2\times0.25=0.50
$$

### Step 4: Add

$$
E[X]=0+0.50+0.50
$$

$$
E[X]=1
$$

We expect one click per comparable two-video recommendation experiment, on average.

### Important interpretation

Expected Value does not guarantee a particular outcome.

A user can still click 0, 1, or 2 videos.

The expected value represents the distribution's mean.

## 6.4 Can Expected Value be fractional?

Yes.

Suppose:

| X | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.50 |
| 1 | 0.30 |
| 2 | 0.20 |

Calculate:

$$
E[X]=0(0.50)+1(0.30)+2(0.20)
$$

$$
=0+0.30+0.40
$$

$$
E[X]=0.70
$$

A user cannot click 0.7 videos during one experiment.

But the average number of clicks across many comparable experiments can be 0.7.

## 6.5 Why do we need Variance?

Imagine two recommendation systems.

Both produce an expected value of one click.

But:

- System A consistently gets one click.
- System B frequently gets zero clicks or two clicks.

Same average, different variability.

Variance describes how spread out a random variable is around its expected value.

## 6.6 Variance formula

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-E[X])^2]
$$

For a discrete variable:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_x (x-\mu)^2P(X=x)
$$

where:

$$
\mu=E[X]
$$

## 6.7 Worked Variance example

Our original PMF:

| X | Probability |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.25 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.25 |

We already calculated:

$$
E[X]=1
$$

Calculate squared deviations.

### X = 0

$$
(0-1)^2=1
$$

Weighted deviation:

$$
1\times0.25=0.25
$$

### X = 1

$$
(1-1)^2=0
$$

Weighted deviation:

$$
0\times0.50=0
$$

### X = 2

$$
(2-1)^2=1
$$

Weighted deviation:

$$
1\times0.25=0.25
$$

Add:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.25+0+0.25
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.50
$$

Variance is 0.5 clicks squared.

## 6.8 Standard Deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance.

$$
SD(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
SD(X)=\sqrt{0.50}
$$

$$
SD(X)\approx0.707
$$

Standard deviation is expressed in the original unit of measurement.

## 6.9 Shortcut Variance formula

Another useful formula:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2
$$

Let's verify.

Calculate:

$$
E[X^2]=0^2(0.25)+1^2(0.50)+2^2(0.25)
$$

$$
=0+0.50+1.00
$$

$$
=1.50
$$

Now:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=1.50-1^2
$$

$$
=0.50
$$

Same answer.

## 6.10 Expected Value for continuous variables

For continuous variables, we integrate instead of summing.

$$
E[T]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t f_T(t)\,dt
$$

Our uniform watch-time example ranges from 0 to 10 minutes.

Because it is symmetric around 5 minutes:

$$
E[T]=5
$$

We can verify using calculus:

$$
E[T]=\int_0^{10}0.1t\,dt
$$

$$
=0.1\left[\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^{10}
$$

$$
=0.1\times50=5
$$

### Production takeaway

Averages alone are insufficient for system design.

Two systems may have identical mean request counts or mean latencies but very different variability and tail behavior.

### Interview question

**Q: Can expected value be outside the set of exact outcomes?**

Yes. For example, expected clicks can be 0.7 even when each individual count must be an integer.

# Lesson 07 — Bernoulli Distribution

## 7.1 Why do we need Bernoulli?

Suppose YouTube recommends one video.

The user either:

- Clicks
- Does not click

These are two possible outcomes.

Define:

$$
X=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{Click} \\
0 & \text{No Click}
\end{cases}
$$

Suppose the probability of clicking is 30%.

$$
P(X=1)=0.30
$$

Then:

$$
P(X=0)=0.70
$$

This is a Bernoulli distribution.

## 7.2 Definition

A Bernoulli distribution models a single experiment with two possible outcomes, typically represented by 0 and 1.

The parameter p represents:

$$
p=P(X=1)
$$

Its complement is:

$$
1-p=P(X=0)
$$

We write:

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)
$$

For our example:

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.30)
$$

## 7.3 Bernoulli PMF

The PMF is:

$$
P(X=x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}
$$

for:

$$
x\in\{0,1\}
$$

### Verify for X = 1

$$
P(X=1)=p^1(1-p)^0
$$

$$
=p
$$

### Verify for X = 0

$$
P(X=0)=p^0(1-p)^1
$$

$$
=1-p
$$

The formula represents both cases in one expression.

## 7.4 Expected Value of Bernoulli

Recall:

$$
E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x)
$$

There are only two values.

$$
E[X]=0(1-p)+1(p)
$$

$$
E[X]=p
$$

Therefore:

$$
E[X]=p
$$

For p = 0.30:

$$
E[X]=0.30
$$

This represents 0.3 expected clicks per impression.

Across 1,000 comparable impressions, the expected number of clicks is:

$$
1000\times0.30=300
$$

This is an expected total, not a guaranteed actual total.

## 7.5 Variance of Bernoulli

Bernoulli variance is:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)
$$

For our example:

$$
=0.30\times0.70
$$

$$
=0.21
$$

### Why this formula makes sense

When p = 0:

The outcome is always 0.

Variance is 0.

When p = 1:

The outcome is always 1.

Variance is 0.

When p = 0.5:

Both outcomes are equally likely.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.5(0.5)=0.25
$$

This is the maximum possible variance of a Bernoulli random variable.

## 7.6 Connection to Logistic Regression

Recall Logistic Regression.

It predicts a probability through the sigmoid function.

$$
p=\sigma(w^Tx+b)
$$

For a binary label Y:

$$
Y\mid X=x\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p(x))
$$

Logistic Regression models the conditional probability that the label equals 1.

### Binary Cross-Entropy connection

The Bernoulli likelihood of observing a label y is:

$$
P(Y=y)=p^y(1-p)^{1-y}
$$

Taking the negative logarithm gives:

$$
L=-[y\log(p)+(1-y)\log(1-p)]
$$

This is Binary Cross-Entropy loss.

### Numerical example

Suppose the actual label is 1.

Model A predicts:

$$
p=0.8
$$

Its loss is:

$$
-\log(0.8)\approx0.223
$$

Model B predicts:

$$
p=0.1
$$

Its loss is:

$$
-\log(0.1)\approx2.303
$$

The confidently incorrect prediction receives a much greater penalty.

### Production consideration

Predicted probabilities aren't automatically well calibrated.

If a model predicts 0.8 for many observations, we'd want roughly 80% of those outcomes to be positive if the probabilities are well calibrated.

That needs to be evaluated.

### Interview question

A transaction is fraudulent with probability 0.02.

Find the probability that it is legitimate.

$$
P(X=0)=1-0.02=0.98
$$

Answer: 98%.

# Lesson 08 — Binomial Distribution

## 8.1 From Bernoulli to Binomial

Bernoulli models one binary trial.

But imagine YouTube recommends three videos.

Each video has a 50% click probability.

Our manager asks:

"What's the probability of exactly two clicks?"

Now we have multiple binary trials.

We want to count the number of successes.

This is a Binomial distribution problem.

## 8.2 Manual calculation

Represent:

- C = Click
- N = No Click

Three recommendations produce eight possible sequences.

| Sequence | Clicks |
|---|---|
| NNN | 0 |
| CNN | 1 |
| NCN | 1 |
| NNC | 1 |
| CCN | 2 |
| CNC | 2 |
| NCC | 2 |
| CCC | 3 |

Assume each click is independent, with probability 0.5.

Every sequence has probability:

$$
0.5\times0.5\times0.5=0.125
$$

Exactly two clicks occur in:

- CCN
- CNC
- NCC

There are three favorable sequences.

Therefore:

$$
P(X=2)=3\times0.125
$$

$$
=0.375
$$

Probability = 37.5%.

## 8.3 Binomial formula

$$
P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

Where:

- n = Number of trials
- k = Number of successes
- p = Success probability in each trial
- 1-p = Failure probability
- The combination term counts arrangements of successes.

Combination formula:

$$
\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
$$

## 8.4 Worked calculation

For our example:

$$
n=3
$$

$$
k=2
$$

$$
p=0.5
$$

Substitute:

$$
P(X=2)=\binom32(0.5)^2(0.5)^1
$$

Calculate combinations:

$$
\binom32=\frac{3!}{2!1!}=3
$$

Calculate the remaining probability:

$$
(0.5)^2(0.5)^1=0.125
$$

Multiply:

$$
3\times0.125=0.375
$$

Answer = 37.5%.

## 8.5 Another example: Ten recommendations

Suppose we recommend ten videos.

Each independently has a 30% click probability.

Find the probability of exactly three clicks.

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,0.30)
$$

$$
P(X=3)=\binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7
$$

Calculate combinations:

$$
\binom{10}{3}=120
$$

Calculate powers:

$$
0.3^3=0.027
$$

$$
0.7^7\approx0.0823543
$$

Multiply:

$$
P(X=3)=120\times0.027\times0.0823543
$$

$$
P(X=3)\approx0.2668
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=3)\approx26.68\%
$$

## 8.6 Binomial Expected Value

If X follows Binomial(n,p):

$$
E[X]=np
$$

For ten recommendations with p = 0.3:

$$
E[X]=10\times0.3=3
$$

The expected number of clicks is 3.

## 8.7 Binomial Variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p)
$$

For our example:

$$
=10(0.3)(0.7)
$$

$$
=2.1
$$

## 8.8 Assumptions of Binomial Distribution

A standard Binomial distribution assumes:

1. A fixed number of trials.
2. Exactly two outcome categories per trial.
3. Independent trials.
4. The same probability of success for every trial.

### When does it fail in production?

Suppose user A has click probability 0.9, while user B has probability 0.1.

If we combine independent trials with different probabilities, the count generally does not follow an ordinary Binomial distribution.

Another possible issue:

A user's click on one recommendation may influence subsequent clicks.

That violates independence.

### Bernoulli vs Binomial

| Bernoulli | Binomial |
|---|---|
| One trial | Multiple fixed trials |
| Outcome: 0 or 1 | Outcome: Number of successes |
| Parameter: p | Parameters: n and p |
| One recommendation click | Clicks across n recommendations |

### Interview question

Four independent recommendations each have a 50% chance of being clicked.

Find the probability of exactly two clicks.

$$
P(X=2)=\binom42(0.5)^2(0.5)^2
$$

$$
=6\times0.0625
$$

$$
=0.375
$$

Answer: 37.5%.

# Lesson 09 — Poisson Distribution

## 9.1 Why do we need Poisson?

Imagine we're monitoring a backend API service.

It receives an average of two requests per second.

Our manager asks:

"What's the probability of receiving exactly three requests in the next second?"

We are no longer counting successes among a predetermined number of trials.

Instead, we're counting events arriving during a time interval.

A Poisson distribution can model such counts under suitable assumptions.

## 9.2 Definition

A Poisson distribution describes the number of events in a fixed interval when arrivals follow a constant-rate Poisson process.

Define:

$$
X=\text{Number of API requests in one second}
$$

Let:

$$
\lambda=2
$$

Here, lambda is the expected number of events in the selected interval.

We write:

$$
X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(2)
$$

## 9.3 Rate vs expected count

Be careful with units.

Suppose the rate is:

$$
r=2\text{ requests/second}
$$

For a one-second window:

$$
\lambda=2\times1=2
$$

For a five-second window:

$$
\lambda=2\times5=10
$$

General relationship:

$$
\lambda=r\times t
$$

The Poisson expected count depends on the interval length.

## 9.4 Poisson PMF

$$
P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}
$$

Where:

- k = Exact number of events
- lambda = Expected number of events
- e = Approximately 2.71828
- k! = Factorial

## 9.5 Worked example: Exactly three requests

Given:

$$
\lambda=2
$$

$$
k=3
$$

Substitute:

$$
P(X=3)=\frac{e^{-2}2^3}{3!}
$$

Calculate:

$$
e^{-2}\approx0.135335
$$

$$
2^3=8
$$

$$
3!=6
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=3)=\frac{0.135335\times8}{6}
$$

$$
\approx0.18045
$$

Probability = approximately 18.04%.

## 9.6 Probability of zero requests

Our manager asks:

"What's the probability of receiving no requests during the next second?"

Set:

$$
k=0
$$

Then:

$$
P(X=0)=\frac{e^{-2}2^0}{0!}
$$

Remember:

$$
2^0=1
$$

$$
0!=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(X=0)=e^{-2}
$$

$$
\approx0.1353
$$

Answer: 13.53%.

## 9.7 Probability of at least one request

Use the Complement Rule.

$$
P(X\geq1)=1-P(X=0)
$$

$$
=1-0.1353
$$

$$
=0.8647
$$

Answer: 86.47%.

## 9.8 Poisson Expected Value and Variance

For a Poisson distribution:

$$
E[X]=\lambda
$$

And:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda
$$

If lambda = 2:

- Mean = 2
- Variance = 2
- Standard deviation = square root of 2

This equality of mean and variance is a property of the Poisson model.

It does not mean that every real-world count dataset has equal mean and variance.

## 9.9 Production considerations

A Poisson process assumes an appropriate constant arrival rate and independent increments.

But production traffic often exhibits:

- Sudden spikes
- Correlated requests
- Retry storms
- Time-of-day changes
- Livestream bursts
- Traffic caused by external events

These patterns can violate the assumptions of a simple constant-rate Poisson model.

### Overdispersion

If observed count variance is much larger than the mean, the data may be overdispersed relative to Poisson.

One diagnostic is:

$$
\text{Fano Factor}=\frac{\text{Variance}}{\text{Mean}}
$$

For an ideal Poisson model, this equals 1.

A much larger observed value suggests further investigation.

It does not by itself prove which alternative model should be used.

### Interview question

An API receives an average of three requests per second.

Find the probability of receiving zero requests in the next second.

$$
P(X=0)=e^{-3}
$$

$$
\approx0.0498
$$

Answer: 4.98%.

# Lesson 10 — Normal Distribution

## 10.1 Why do we need Normal Distribution?

Imagine we're analyzing daily video watch time.

For our learning example, assume watch time follows a Normal distribution.

We have:

- Mean = 60 minutes
- Standard deviation = 10 minutes

Our manager asks:

"How unusual is a user who watches for 80 minutes?"

To answer, we need to understand how values are distributed around the mean.

The Normal distribution provides one model for this.

## 10.2 Definition

A Normal distribution is a continuous, symmetric, bell-shaped probability distribution.

Its properties include:

1. Symmetry around the mean.
2. Mean, median, and mode are equal.
3. The highest density occurs at the mean.
4. The total area under the curve equals 1.
5. Standard deviation determines the spread.

## 10.3 Mathematical notation

$$
X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)
$$

Where:

- mu = Mean
- sigma = Standard deviation
- sigma squared = Variance

Our example:

$$
\mu=60
$$

$$
\sigma=10
$$

Therefore:

$$
\sigma^2=100
$$

Write:

$$
X\sim N(60,100)
$$

**Important:** The second parameter represents variance, not standard deviation.

## 10.4 The 68–95–99.7 Rule

For a Normal distribution:

Approximately 68% of observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean.

Approximately 95% fall within two standard deviations.

Approximately 99.7% fall within three standard deviations.

### Our example

Mean = 60

Standard deviation = 10

| Range | Interval | Approximate probability |
|---|---|---|
| Mean ± 1 SD | 50 to 70 | 68% |
| Mean ± 2 SD | 40 to 80 | 95% |
| Mean ± 3 SD | 30 to 90 | 99.7% |

### Interpretation

Suppose the model describes a population of 10,000 comparable users.

We would expect approximately:

- 6,800 users between 50 and 70 minutes.
- 9,500 users between 40 and 80 minutes.
- 9,970 users between 30 and 90 minutes.

These are model-based expected proportions, not guaranteed observed counts.

## 10.5 Z-Score

A Z-score expresses how far a value is from the mean in standard-deviation units.

Formula:

$$
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
$$

Suppose a user watches 80 minutes.

Given:

$$
x=80
$$

$$
\mu=60
$$

$$
\sigma=10
$$

Calculate:

$$
z=\frac{80-60}{10}
$$

$$
z=2
$$

This user is two standard deviations above the mean.

## 10.6 Convert Z-score into probability

For a standard Normal variable Z:

$$
P(Z\leq2)\approx0.97725
$$

This means approximately 97.725% of values lie at or below Z = 2.

Our manager asks:

"What percentage of users watch longer than 80 minutes?"

We need the upper-tail probability.

$$
P(X>80)=1-P(Z\leq2)
$$

$$
=1-0.97725
$$

$$
=0.02275
$$

Answer: Approximately 2.28%.

### Approximation vs precise calculation

The 68–95–99.7 rule suggests about 2.5% beyond +2 SD.

A standard Normal CDF gives approximately 2.28%.

The first is a rough estimate.

The second is a more accurate probability.

## 10.7 Probability between two values

Find:

$$
P(50\lt X\lt80)
$$

Convert the first endpoint to a Z-score:

$$
z_1=\frac{50-60}{10}=-1
$$

Convert the second:

$$
z_2=\frac{80-60}{10}=2
$$

Use standard Normal CDF values:

$$
P(Z\leq2)=0.97725
$$

$$
P(Z\leq-1)=0.15866
$$

Subtract:

$$
P(50\lt X\lt80)=0.97725-0.15866
$$

$$
=0.81859
$$

Answer: Approximately 81.86%.

This is another application of the CDF difference formula.

## 10.8 Normal PDF formula

$$
f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
$$

Intuition:

- The difference x minus mu measures distance from the center.
- Squaring makes the density symmetric.
- Sigma controls the spread.
- The exponential term causes density to decrease with distance.
- The normalization factor makes total area equal 1.

We don't need to calculate this expression manually for every probability.

Z-scores and the standard Normal CDF are usually more convenient.

## 10.9 When Normal is inappropriate

Real watch time may be:

- Strongly right-skewed
- Limited below by zero
- A mixture of several user groups
- Heavy-tailed

A Normal distribution assigns some probability to negative values.

For strictly nonnegative variables, that can be a serious modeling limitation.

Possible alternatives for positive continuous measurements include:

- Lognormal
- Gamma
- Other empirically suitable distributions

### Central Limit Theorem preview

The averages of sufficiently large samples can often be approximately Normal under appropriate assumptions.

This does not imply the original individual observations are Normal.

We'll study that distinction in Statistical Inference.

### Interview questions

**Q: If X follows N(60,100), what is its standard deviation?**

$$
\sqrt{100}=10
$$

**Q: What does Z = -2 mean?**

The observation is two standard deviations below the mean.

# Lesson 11 — Exponential Distribution

## 11.1 Why do we need Exponential Distribution?

Remember the Poisson example?

Our API receives an average of two requests per second.

Poisson answered:

"How many requests arrive during one second?"

But what if the manager asks:

"How long will we wait until the next request arrives?"

Now we're measuring time instead of counting events.

This leads us to the Exponential distribution.

## 11.2 Definition

An Exponential distribution models the waiting time until the next event in a constant-rate Poisson process.

Define:

$$
T=\text{Time until the next API request}
$$

Suppose the arrival rate is:

$$
r=2\text{ requests/second}
$$

Then:

$$
T\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(r=2)
$$

Many textbooks use lambda instead of r for the rate parameter.

## 11.3 Expected waiting time

For the rate parameterization:

$$
E[T]=\frac1r
$$

Our rate is:

$$
r=2
$$

Therefore:

$$
E[T]=\frac12
$$

$$
E[T]=0.5\text{ seconds}
$$

The expected waiting time is half a second.

This does not mean every request arrives exactly 0.5 seconds apart.

The actual waiting time is random.

## 11.4 Different arrival rate

Suppose our API now receives four requests per second.

$$
r=4
$$

Then:

$$
E[T]=\frac14
$$

$$
=0.25\text{ seconds}
$$

That's 250 milliseconds.

Higher arrival rate implies lower expected waiting time under this model.

## 11.5 Probability of waiting longer than a threshold

Suppose we ask:

"What's the probability that the next request takes more than one second to arrive?"

For Exponential waiting time:

$$
P(T>t)=e^{-rt}
$$

Given:

$$
r=2
$$

$$
t=1
$$

Substitute:

$$
P(T>1)=e^{-2(1)}
$$

$$
=e^{-2}
$$

$$
\approx0.1353
$$

Answer: Approximately 13.53%.

## 11.6 Connection to Poisson

Why does this work?

Waiting longer than one second means no events arrived during that second.

From the Poisson distribution:

$$
P(N(1)=0)=e^{-2}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(T>1)=P(N(1)=0)
$$

Both are approximately 13.53%.

This is the relationship between Poisson event counts and Exponential waiting times.

## 11.7 Probability of arrival within a threshold

Suppose we ask:

"What's the probability that at least one request arrives within one second?"

Use the Complement Rule.

$$
P(T\leq1)=1-P(T>1)
$$

$$
=1-e^{-2}
$$

$$
\approx0.8647
$$

Answer: Approximately 86.47%.

## 11.8 Exponential CDF

For nonnegative t:

$$
F_T(t)=1-e^{-rt}
$$

This gives the probability that the next event occurs within t time units.

The complete CDF is:

$$
F_T(t)=
\begin{cases}
0 & t\lt0 \\
1-e^{-rt} & t\geq0
\end{cases}
$$

## 11.9 Exponential PDF

For nonnegative t:

$$
f_T(t)=re^{-rt}
$$

This is the density of waiting time.

As with every continuous random variable, probability comes from area over an interval.

## 11.10 Probability between waiting times

Suppose we want:

"The probability that the next request arrives after 0.2 seconds but within 0.7 seconds."

Our arrival rate remains:

$$
r=2
$$

Using the CDF:

$$
P(0.2\lt T\leq0.7)=F_T(0.7)-F_T(0.2)
$$

Substitute:

$$
=(1-e^{-1.4})-(1-e^{-0.4})
$$

Simplify:

$$
=e^{-0.4}-e^{-1.4}
$$

Approximate:

$$
=0.67032-0.24660
$$

$$
=0.42372
$$

Answer: Approximately 42.37%.

## 11.11 Exponential Variance

For the rate parameterization:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac1{r^2}
$$

And:

$$
SD(T)=\frac1r
$$

For r = 2:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac14=0.25
$$

$$
SD(T)=0.5\text{ seconds}
$$

## 11.12 Memoryless Property

The Exponential distribution has a special property called memorylessness.

Suppose we've already waited two seconds without receiving a request.

Does that make a request more likely to arrive in the next second?

Under the Exponential model, no.

The additional waiting-time distribution remains unchanged.

Mathematically:

$$
P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=P(T>t)
$$

Interpretation:

Given that we've already waited s time units, the probability of waiting at least another t time units is the same as when we started.

This property depends on the model assumptions.

## 11.13 Rate vs Scale

Some software libraries parameterize the Exponential distribution using scale instead of rate.

Rate:

$$
r=2\text{ events/second}
$$

Scale:

$$
\theta=\frac1r=0.5\text{ seconds}
$$

Both describe the same distribution when interpreted correctly.

Always check the library's parameterization.

### Production considerations

Actual request arrivals can exhibit bursts and changing rates.

The Exponential model may therefore be inappropriate without validation.

Also distinguish:

- Interarrival time
- Request processing time
- End-to-end response latency

They measure different quantities.

### Interview question

An API receives four requests per second.

Find the expected time until the next request.

$$
E[T]=\frac14=0.25\text{ seconds}
$$

Answer: 250 milliseconds.

# Production Engineering: Choosing a Distribution

Knowing formulas is not enough for real-world Data Science.

Before choosing a distribution, ask these questions.

## 1. What exactly does the random variable measure?

Examples:

- Clicks per impression
- Clicks per user per day
- Requests per second
- Latency per request

These are different variables.

Do not mix their distributions.

## 2. What is the observation window?

For request counts:

- Per second
- Per minute
- Per hour

The distribution's parameters may change with the window.

## 3. Is the variable discrete or continuous?

Counts are usually discrete.

Measured quantities such as elapsed time are usually modeled as continuous.

## 4. Are observations independent?

Examples of possible dependence:

- Multiple clicks from the same user
- Retries generated by the same failing request
- Recommendations affected by previous clicks

Dependence can invalidate simple textbook models.

## 5. Are model parameters stable?

A constant arrival-rate assumption may fail when traffic changes dramatically during a livestream.

A constant click probability may fail when user populations differ.

## 6. Does the distribution's support make sense?

A Normal distribution can assign probability to negative values.

That may be problematic for strictly nonnegative measurements.

A Binomial count cannot exceed n.

A Poisson count has no fixed upper limit.

The possible values must match the real quantity.

## 7. Have we validated the model?

Useful checks include:

- Histograms
- Empirical cumulative distributions
- Distribution fitting diagnostics
- Mean and variance comparisons
- Tail probabilities
- Temporal stability
- Out-of-sample validation

A model is useful only when its assumptions and predictions are appropriate for the problem.

# Important Interview Traps

### 1. Is a PDF height equal to probability?

No. Probability is the area under the PDF over an interval.

### 2. Can PDF height exceed 1?

Yes. Total area must equal 1.

### 3. Can a CDF decrease?

No. It is nondecreasing.

### 4. Can the expected value be fractional when outcomes are integers?

Yes. Expected value is a weighted average.

### 5. Can variance be negative?

No. Variance is a nonnegative expectation of squared deviations.

### 6. Does Bernoulli success mean a desirable outcome?

No. It means the event coded as 1.

### 7. Does Binomial always work when counting clicks?

No. Its assumptions include independent trials with a common success probability.

### 8. Does Poisson always work for API request counts?

No. Constant-rate and independence assumptions must be considered.

### 9. Does Normal mean the data must contain negative values?

No. But the theoretical Normal distribution does assign some probability to negative values.

### 10. Are individual observations Normal whenever their sample mean is approximately Normal?

No. Sampling distributions and individual-observation distributions are different concepts.

### 11. Is Exponential interarrival time the same as API processing time?

No. They measure different quantities.

### 12. Is Exponential memoryless?

Yes, under its distributional assumptions.

\operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)
$$

## Binomial PMF

$$
P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

## Binomial Mean and Variance

$$
E[X]=np
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p)
$$

## Poisson PMF

$$
P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}
$$

## Poisson Mean and Variance

$$
E[X]=\lambda
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda
$$

## Normal Z-Score

$$
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
$$

## Exponential Expected Waiting Time

$$
E[T]=\frac1r
$$

## Exponential Survival Probability

$$
P(T>t)=e^{-rt}
$$

## Exponential CDF

$$
F_T(t)=1-e^{-rt}
$$

## Exponential PDF

$$
f_T(t)=re^{-rt}
$$

# Final Chapter Takeaway

Probability distributions allow us to reason mathematically about uncertain outcomes.

Always begin by understanding:

1. What is the random variable?
2. Is it discrete or continuous?
3. What possible values can it take?
4. Are we interested in an exact outcome, an interval, or a cumulative probability?
5. What is its expected value?
6. How much does it vary?
7. Which probability distribution is a reasonable candidate?
8. Are that distribution's assumptions suitable for the real-world system?

For Machine Learning interviews, understanding these questions is more valuable than memorizing formulas alone.

For production Data Science, choosing a mathematically convenient distribution is not enough. The distribution must adequately represent the actual data and the decisions being made.
