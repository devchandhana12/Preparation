## 1. Random Experiments, Outcomes, Sample Spaces, and Events

### Intuition

A **random experiment** is an action whose result is uncertain before it happens, such as rolling a die or showing a video recommendation to a user.

- **Outcome:** One possible result. A die shows `4`.
- **Sample space (`S`):** All possible results. For a die, `S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}`.
- **Event:** A condition that one or more outcomes satisfy.

### Worked example: Roll an even number

Let `A = {2, 4, 6}` be the event that a fair die shows an even number.

1. Count all possible outcomes: `6`.
2. Count favorable outcomes: `3` (`2`, `4`, `6`).
3. Because a **fair** die makes the six outcomes equally likely:

$$
P(A)=\frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}{\text{total equally likely outcomes}}=\frac36=0.5=50\%.
$$

**Interpretation:** Half the possible outcomes satisfy the condition; it does **not** guarantee every second roll will be even.

**When the counting formula fails:** If a die is biased, the six faces are not equally likely. We must use the probabilities assigned to the faces, not simply `3/6`.

**Production link:** “What fraction of recommendation *impressions* get clicked?” and “What fraction of *users* click at least once?” are different experiments. Define the unit before calculating probability.

## 2. Mutually Exclusive Events

### Intuition

Two events are **mutually exclusive** when they cannot both occur in the same trial.

### Worked example: One die roll

- `A = {2, 4, 6}`: rolling an even number.
- `B = {1, 3, 5}`: rolling an odd number.

No face appears in both sets, so their **intersection** is empty:

$$
A\cap B=\varnothing,\qquad P(A\cap B)=0.
$$

**Contrast:** Let `C = {4, 5, 6}` be rolling more than 3. Then:

$$
A\cap C=\{4,6\},\qquad P(A\cap C)=\frac26=\frac13.
$$

So **even** and **greater than 3** are **not** mutually exclusive; rolling 4 satisfies both.

**Interview trap:** Mutually exclusive does **not** mean independent. Learning that a roll was even reduces the probability of odd to `0`.

---

## 3. Collectively Exhaustive Events

### Intuition

A collection of events is **collectively exhaustive** when together they cover **every** possible outcome.

### Worked example

For `S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}`:

- `A = {1, 2, 3}`
- `B = {4, 5, 6}`

Then:

$$
A\cup B=S,\qquad P(A\cup B)=1.
$$

Their **union** (`∪`) is all outcomes that belong to A, B, **or both**. Here there is no overlap, so these events are both mutually exclusive **and** exhaustive.

**Not exhaustive:** `{1, 2, 3}` and `{4, 5}` miss outcome `6`.

**Exhaustive but overlapping:** `{1, 2, 3, 4}` and `{4, 5, 6}` cover the sample space but share `4`.

**Remember:**

| Property | Question |
|---|---|
| Mutually exclusive | Can the events occur together? **No** |
| Exhaustive | Is any possible outcome missing? **No** |

## 4. Independent Events

### Intuition

Events A and B are **independent** when knowing that A happened does not change the probability of B.

### Worked example: Two fair coin tosses

Let:

- `A`: first toss is Heads; `P(A) = 1/2`.
- `B`: second toss is Heads; `P(B) = 1/2`.

All equally likely pairs are `HH, HT, TH, TT`; only `HH` satisfies both.

For independent events:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)=\frac12\times\frac12=\frac14=25\%.
$$

The result of the first toss does not change the second toss's chance of Heads.

Equivalently, when `P(A) > 0`:

$$
P(B\mid A)=P(B).
$$

### Why mutually exclusive is different

On **one die roll**, even and odd are mutually exclusive, but:

- `P(odd) = 1/2`.
- `P(odd | even) = 0`.

The probability changed, so they are **dependent**, not independent.

**Important:** Two events with positive probabilities cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent.

## 5. Dependent Events

### Intuition

Events are dependent when knowing the outcome of one changes the probability of the other.

### Worked example: Draw without replacement

A bag contains **3 red balls and 2 blue balls**. Draw twice **without putting the first ball back**.

We want **Red on the first draw AND Red on the second draw**.

1. First draw: `3` red out of `5` balls.

   $$P(R_1)=\frac35.$$

2. **Assuming the first ball was red**, the bag now has `2` red out of `4` remaining balls.

   $$P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac24.$$

3. Multiply the first step by the conditional second step:

   $$
   P(R_1\cap R_2)=\frac35\times\frac24=\frac6{20}=0.30=30\%.
   $$

### What if we replace the first ball?

After putting it back, the bag again has 3 red out of 5. The draws are then independent (under identical random-draw conditions):

$$
P(R_1\cap R_2)=\frac35\times\frac35=\frac9{25}=36\%.
$$

**Takeaway:** “With replacement” versus “without replacement” changes the second probability.

## 6. Conditional Probability — GIVEN

### Running YouTube dataset

We observed **100 users**:

| User type | Clicked | Did not click | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Premium | 20 | 20 | 40 |
| Free | 15 | 45 | 60 |
| **Total** | **35** | **65** | **100** |

### Question A: What is P(Click | Premium)?

**Given Premium** means we only consider the 40 Premium users — **not** all 100 users.

Among those 40, 20 clicked:

$$
P(Click\mid Premium)=\frac{20}{40}=50\%.
$$

### Question B: What is P(Premium | Click)?

**Given Click** means we only consider the 35 users who clicked. Among those 35, 20 were Premium:

$$
P(Premium\mid Click)=\frac{20}{35}\approx57.14\%.
$$

**Same dataset, different denominator, different answer.**

### General formula and why it works

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\quad P(B)>0.
$$

The numerator counts outcomes meeting **both** conditions; the denominator counts outcomes meeting the condition **after the bar**.

For Click given Premium:

$$
\frac{P(Click\cap Premium)}{P(Premium)}
=\frac{20/100}{40/100}=\frac{20}{40}=0.5.
$$

**Interview trap:** `P(A | B)` is generally **not** equal to `P(B | A)`.

## 7. General Multiplication Rule — AND

### Intuition

If a question says **A AND B**, we want the probability that *both* conditions are true.

### Worked YouTube example

**Question:** Select one of our 100 users. What's the chance that this user is **Premium AND clicked**?

From the table, we know the direct answer is `20/100 = 20%`. Now derive it step by step:

1. Chance of Premium: `40/100`.
2. Given Premium, chance of Click: `20/40`.
3. Multiply:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Premium\cap Click)
&=P(Premium)P(Click\mid Premium)\\
&=\frac{40}{100}\times\frac{20}{40}\\
&=\frac{20}{100}=20\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

### General formula

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).
$$

You can reverse the order too:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B).
$$

**Do not automatically multiply `P(A) × P(B)`** — that shortcut is valid only when A and B are independent.

In our data, `P(Premium) × P(Click) = 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.14`, which is **wrong** for the joint event because the observed joint probability is `0.20`.

## 8. Addition Rule — OR

### Intuition

**A OR B** includes outcomes satisfying A, B, **or both**. If we simply add `P(A)` and `P(B)`, the overlap gets counted twice.

### Worked YouTube example

**Question:** What's the probability a user is **Premium OR clicked**?

From our 100 users:

- Premium users: `40`.
- Clicked users: `35`.
- Premium **and** clicked (already in both counts): `20`.

Unique users meeting at least one condition:

$$
40+35-20=55.
$$

So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Premium\cup Click)
&=P(Premium)+P(Click)-P(Premium\cap Click)\\
&=0.40+0.35-0.20\\
&=0.55=55\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why subtract?** The 20 Premium clickers were included once in the count of Premium users and again in the count of clickers.

### Special case: Mutually exclusive events

On one fair die roll, the event **roll 2 OR roll 5** has no overlap:

$$
P(2\text{ OR }5)=\frac16+\frac16=\frac26=\frac13.
$$

The intersection is zero, so there is nothing to subtract.

## 9. Complement Rule — NOT

### Intuition

The complement of A, written `Aᶜ`, is everything in the sample space **not** in A.

### Worked YouTube example

Our dataset has `35` users who clicked out of `100`.

- `P(Click) = 35/100 = 0.35`.
- `P(No Click) = 65/100 = 0.65`.

But we can get the second value without recounting:

$$
P(A^c)=1-P(A).
$$

So:

$$
P(No\ Click)=1-0.35=0.65=65\%.
$$

### Worked API example

If 99.8% of requests succeed, and every request is categorized as success or failure:

$$
P(Failure)=1-0.998=0.002=0.2\%.
$$

**Important:** Define the event precisely. If requests can also be “cancelled” or “unknown,” make sure those outcomes are accounted for before treating failure as the complement of success.

## 10. Law of Total Probability & Probability Trees

### Intuition

When an event can happen through several **non-overlapping paths** covering the whole population, calculate the probability along each path and add the results.

### Worked YouTube example

We want the **overall probability of a click** using only group probabilities:

- `P(Premium) = 40/100 = 0.40`.
- `P(Click | Premium) = 20/40 = 0.50`.
- `P(Free) = 60/100 = 0.60`.
- `P(Click | Free) = 15/60 = 0.25`.

The two routes are:

```text
                    Random user
                    /         \
            Premium (0.40)   Free (0.60)
              /      \        /      \
       Click(.50)  No(.50) Click(.25) No(.75)
          |                    |
     0.40×0.50            0.60×0.25
        =0.20                =0.15
```

**Multiply down each path:**

- Premium and Click: `0.40 × 0.50 = 0.20`.
- Free and Click: `0.60 × 0.25 = 0.15`.

**Add the paths leading to Click:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Click)
&=P(Premium)P(Click\mid Premium)\\
&\quad+P(Free)P(Click\mid Free)\\
&=0.40(0.50)+0.60(0.25)\\
&=0.20+0.15=\boxed{0.35=35\%}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The result agrees with our table: `35` out of `100` clicked.

### General form

If groups `G₁, G₂, ..., Gₖ` are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive:

$$
P(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(G_i)P(C\mid G_i).
$$

**Memory aid:** **Multiply along a path, add across valid paths.**

## 11. Bayes' Theorem — Reverse GIVEN

### The question

We know `P(Click | Premium)` = **50%**. But after learning that a user **clicked**, what is the probability that the user is **Premium**?

The condition has reversed.

### Solution from the table (intuition first)

- There are `35` users who clicked.
- Of these, `20` are Premium.

Therefore:

$$
P(Premium\mid Click)=\frac{20}{35}\approx57.14\%.
$$

### Deriving Bayes' theorem

Recall two equally valid ways to calculate an intersection:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)
$$

and

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B).
$$

Set them equal and rearrange:

$$
P(B)P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)
$$

$$
\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}}.
$$

### Worked YouTube calculation

Choose `A = Premium` and `B = Click`:

| Bayes term | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Prior `P(A)` | Chance of Premium before learning about the click | `0.40` |
| Likelihood `P(B | A)` | Click probability for Premium users | `0.50` |
| Evidence `P(B)` | Overall click probability | `0.35` |
| Posterior `P(A | B)` | Chance of Premium after learning they clicked | `?` |

Substitute:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Premium\mid Click)
&=\frac{P(Click\mid Premium)P(Premium)}{P(Click)}\\
&=\frac{0.50\times0.40}{0.35}\\
&=\frac{0.20}{0.35}\\
&\approx0.5714=57.14\%.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Interpretation:** Before knowing whether the user clicked, the chance of Premium was 40%. After observing a click, it increases to about 57.14%, because clicking is more common among Premium users in this example.

### Why this matters in ML: Fraud example

Imagine only **1% of transactions are fraudulent**. A fraud alert system flags:

- 90% of fraudulent transactions (true-positive rate).
- 5% of legitimate transactions (false-positive rate).

Among 10,000 transactions, we'd expect:

| Actual status | Total | Flagged |
|---|---:|---:|
| Fraud | 100 | 90 |
| Legitimate | 9,900 | 495 |
| **Total flagged** | | **585** |

Given a flagged transaction, probability it is truly fraud:

$$
P(Fraud\mid Flag)=\frac{90}{585}\approx15.38\%.
$$

Despite detecting 90% of fraud cases, **most alerts in this example are false positives** because fraud is rare. This is the **base-rate effect**, and why prior probabilities matter.

## 12. Revision, Interview Problems, and Assessment

### First identify the wording

| Wording | Think | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **AND** | Both happen | `P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B | A)` |
| **OR** | Either or both | `P(A ∪ B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A ∩ B)` |
| **NOT** | Complement | `P(Aᶜ) = 1−P(A)` |
| **GIVEN** | Restrict to known condition | `P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B)` |
| **Several possible paths** | Multiply within, add across paths | Total probability |
| **Reverse GIVEN** | Update probability using evidence | Bayes |

### Seven solved interview-style questions

**Q1. Even vs odd on one die: independent or mutually exclusive?**  
**Answer:** Mutually exclusive but not independent. If even happened, odd is impossible on that roll: `P(Odd | Even) = 0` rather than `P(Odd) = 1/2`.

**Q2. Among 20 users, 8 are Premium; 6 of the Premium users clicked. What is P(Click | Premium)?**  
**Answer:** `6/8 = 75%`. The denominator is **8** because we are *given Premium*.

**Q3. In those 20 users, what is P(Premium AND Click)?**  
**Answer:** `(8/20) × (6/8) = 6/20 = 30%`. We need both conditions out of **all 20** users.

**Q4. P(A) = 45%, P(B) = 35%, overlap = 15%. What is P(A OR B)?**  
**Answer:** `45% + 35% − 15% = 65%`. Subtract the overlap counted twice.

**Q5. API success rate is 97.5%. Failure rate?**  
**Answer:** `1 − 0.975 = 0.025 = 2.5%` if all outcomes are success or failure.

**Q6. 60% Premium, 40% Free. Premium click rate 50%; Free click rate 25%. Overall click probability?**  
**Answer:** `(0.60 × 0.50) + (0.40 × 0.25) = 0.30 + 0.10 = 0.40 = 40%`.

**Q7. 30% Premium; Premium click rate 60%; Free click rate 20%. If someone clicked, how likely are they Premium?**  
First find click probability: `(0.30 × 0.60) + (0.70 × 0.20) = 0.18 + 0.14 = 0.32`.  
Then Bayes: `P(Premium | Click) = (0.60 × 0.30)/0.32 = 0.5625 = 56.25%`.

### Production-level caution

1. **Choose the statistical unit.** Click probability per impression is not necessarily click probability per user.
2. **Avoid selection bias.** Logged users and sampled users must represent the intended population.
3. **Don't invent independence.** Multiple events from the same user can be dependent.
4. **Be explicit about time windows.** “Clicked” must mean clicked in a defined observation window.
5. **Don't confuse association with causation.** Premium and clicks can be associated without Premium membership causing clicks.
6. **Guard against data leakage.** Don't use future outcomes in features meant to predict the past.
7. **Handle rare events with base rates.** An accurate detector can still generate many false positives.

## Final One-Page Memory Map

- **Outcomes** = what can happen; **sample space** = everything that can happen; **event** = what we're asking about.
- **Mutually exclusive** = can't occur together. **Exhaustive** = nothing is missing. **Independent** = knowing one doesn't change the other.
- **Conditional probability** = narrow the denominator to the group *given*.
- **AND** = intersection; multiply with the relevant condition.
- **OR** = union; subtract the overlap.
- **NOT** = subtract from 1.
- **Total probability** = divide the population into complete, non-overlapping groups and sum the event probability across them.
- **Bayes** = reverse a conditional probability using likelihood, prior, and evidence.
